How to get your brand recommended by ChatGPT

More buyers now ask AI tools for recommendations before they ever search Google. Here's how answer engine optimisation (AEO) gets your brand into those answers.

John JayaseelanFounder & CEO, GreyLeads

Ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Google's AI Overviews for "the best digital marketing agency in Bengaluru" or "a good dentist near Indiranagar", and you get a short list of names, not ten blue links. If your brand isn't in that short list, you're invisible to a growing group of buyers, even if you rank well on Google.

That's what answer engine optimisation (AEO) is about: making your brand the answer AI tools are confident enough to recommend.

How AI tools decide who to recommend

AI assistants don't "rank" pages the way Google does. They read what the web says about you and decide whether they can recommend you with confidence. In practice they look for three things:

  • Clarity. Can they easily tell what you do, who you serve and where you're based?
  • Consistency. Do your website, Google Business Profile, LinkedIn and directory listings all say the same thing?
  • Corroboration. Do other trusted sources (reviews, articles, client mentions) back up what you say about yourself?

Step 1: Make your website easy for machines to read

Every important page should answer, in plain words, the questions a buyer would ask. Who is this for? What exactly do you do? Where? How much, roughly? How long does it take?

  • Write a clear one-line description of your business near the top of the homepage.
  • Give each service its own page with a specific title, for example "Website Design in Bangalore", not just "Services".
  • Add an FAQ section that answers real customer questions in two or three sentences each.
  • Add structured data (schema) for your organisation, services, location and FAQs so AI tools don't have to guess.

Step 2: Fix your facts everywhere

If your website says "Bellandur", your Google profile says "Marathahalli" and an old directory lists a phone number you stopped using in 2021, AI tools see conflicting information and play it safe by recommending someone else.

Make a list of every place your business appears online and make the name, address, phone, services and hours identical. Start with your Google Business Profile; it's one of the most-read sources for local answers.

Step 3: Earn mentions beyond your own website

AI tools trust what others say about you more than what you say about yourself. Focus on:

  • Reviews on Google that mention the specific service and city ("great SEO agency in Bangalore").
  • Client stories published on partner and client websites.
  • Industry articles, interviews and podcasts where your team shares expertise.
  • Listings in respected directories for your industry.

Step 4: Publish content that answers real questions

Look at what your sales team gets asked every week and turn each answer into a short, specific article. "How much does a website cost in India in 2026?" is far more useful to an AI tool (and a buyer) than "Why websites matter".

Step 5: Check how AI tools describe you

Once a month, ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity questions your buyers would ask. Note whether you're mentioned, how you're described and which sources are cited. When something is wrong or missing, fix it at the source.

AEO doesn't replace SEO. It builds on it. The brands that win are the ones that are clear, consistent and talked about.

The short version

  1. Say clearly what you do, for whom and where, on every key page.
  2. Keep your business facts identical everywhere online.
  3. Collect reviews and mentions that name your service and city.
  4. Answer real buyer questions in plain language.
  5. Check what AI tools say about you every month.

Why your website gets traffic but no leads

Visitors arrive, look around and leave without a word. Here are the most common reasons, and how to fix each one.

Mani KandanChief Technical Officer, GreyLeads

Your analytics show thousands of visitors a month, but the enquiry inbox is quiet. It's one of the most frustrating problems in marketing, and one of the most common. The good news: it's usually caused by a handful of fixable issues.

1. The wrong people are arriving

Traffic is only useful if it's the right traffic. Broad keywords, untargeted ads or viral social posts can bring visitors who were never going to buy.

Fix: Check where your visitors come from and which pages they land on. Shift budget and content towards searches with buying intent, like "website design company in Bangalore", rather than broad topics.

2. Visitors can't tell what you do in five seconds

If your homepage headline is clever but vague, people leave before scrolling. They should instantly understand what you offer, who it's for and why you're different.

Fix: Rewrite the top of your homepage in plain words. Add one clear action, such as "Get a free quote".

3. The site is slow, especially on mobile

Most Indian buyers browse on their phones, often on patchy connections. Every extra second of loading loses people before they see anything.

Fix: Compress images, remove unused plugins and scripts, and test your site on a mid-range phone using Google's PageSpeed Insights.

4. There's no reason to trust you

Buyers look for proof: client logos, case studies, reviews, real photos of your team and office, and clear contact details. Without them, even interested visitors hesitate.

Fix: Add trust signals near your calls to action, not just on an "About" page buried in the menu.

5. Getting in touch is hard work

Long forms, hidden phone numbers and "contact us" pages with no clear next step all kill enquiries.

Fix: Keep forms short (name, phone, what you need), add a WhatsApp button, and show your phone number in the header on mobile.

6. Nobody follows up fast enough

Sometimes the leads do come in, but they go cold in an inbox. An enquiry answered in minutes is far more likely to turn into a conversation than one answered the next day.

Fix: Route form submissions to WhatsApp or your CRM and send an instant acknowledgement automatically.

A simple checklist

  • Are the right people visiting? Check traffic sources and landing pages.
  • Is it clear what you do within five seconds?
  • Does the site load fast on a phone?
  • Is there visible proof near every call to action?
  • Can someone contact you in under 30 seconds?
  • Does every lead get a reply within minutes?

Fix these six things and the same traffic you already have will start turning into conversations.

The creative testing system behind our best ads

Great targeting can't save a boring ad. Here's the simple, repeatable way we test creative so budget always flows to the winners.

EvangelineMarketing Head, GreyLeads

On today's ad platforms, the algorithm does much of the targeting for you. That makes the creative (the image, video and words) the biggest lever you still control. The brands that grow are the ones that test creative systematically instead of guessing.

Start with angles, not designs

Most teams test small things: a different background colour, a new button. The bigger wins come from testing different angles, the core reason someone should care.

For a solar training course, angles might be: "earn more as a certified installer", "start your own solar business", "learn from engineers in 6 weeks", or "government-backed career". Each angle speaks to a different motivation.

Our four-step testing loop

  1. Brainstorm 4–6 angles from customer interviews, reviews and sales calls.
  2. Make one simple ad per angle, in the same format, so you're testing the message, not the production quality.
  3. Run them side by side with equal budget until each has enough results to compare fairly.
  4. Scale the winner and iterate: make new versions of the winning angle with different hooks, formats and visuals.

What to measure

Clicks are easy to get and easy to misread. We judge creative on what matters to the business:

  • Cost per lead or cost per purchase, not just click-through rate.
  • Lead quality: do these leads pick up the phone and buy?
  • Hook rate for video: how many people watch past the first three seconds?

The first three seconds decide everything

On Reels and Shorts, people decide almost instantly whether to keep watching. Strong hooks usually do one of these:

  • Call out the audience directly ("Bangalore salon owners, this is for you").
  • Show the result first, then explain how.
  • Ask a question the viewer is already asking themselves.
  • Show movement or a surprising visual immediately.

Refresh before fatigue sets in

Even winning ads wear out as the same people see them again and again. Watch for rising costs and falling click rates, and have the next round of creative ready before performance drops.

Keep a creative library

Record every test: the angle, the hook, the format and the result. Over a few months this becomes your most valuable marketing asset, a clear picture of what makes your customers act.

Don't ask "which ad looks best?" Ask "which message makes the right people act?" Then let the numbers answer.

7 ways to cut your cost per lead without cutting quality

Cheaper leads are easy. Cheaper leads that actually buy are harder. Seven practical changes that lower your CPL and keep quality high.

John JayaseelanFounder & CEO, GreyLeads

Lowering your cost per lead (CPL) is easy if you don't care who the leads are. Cheap leads that never pick up the phone are worse than no leads at all: they waste your sales team's time. Here's how to bring costs down while keeping quality high.

1. Fix the offer before the ads

A weak offer is expensive to advertise. "Contact us for a quote" asks for effort and gives nothing back. "Get a free 15-minute website audit" or "Download our price guide" gives a clear reason to act.

2. Send ads to a dedicated landing page

Sending paid traffic to your homepage spreads attention across every menu link. A focused landing page with one message and one action usually converts far better.

3. Tighten your targeting with real data

Upload your list of past customers to build lookalike audiences, exclude existing customers and job seekers, and focus on the locations you actually serve, like specific areas of Bengaluru and Chennai rather than all of India.

4. Use negative keywords on Google

Words like "free", "jobs", "course", "salary" or "DIY" often attract the wrong searches. Review your search terms every week and exclude the ones that never convert.

5. Add a little friction, on purpose

One extra qualifying question, like budget range, city or timeline, may slightly reduce the number of leads, but it raises their quality and lets your team prioritise. Fewer, better leads often means a lower cost per customer.

6. Test creative constantly

The single biggest driver of cost on Meta and YouTube is the creative. Test different angles and hooks, then move budget to the winners every week.

7. Follow up faster

A lead contacted within minutes is far more likely to convert than one called back the next day. Instant WhatsApp replies, automatic emails and alerts to your sales team turn more of the leads you already pay for into customers.

Measure the right number

CPL matters, but the number that really counts is cost per customer. Connect your ads to your CRM so you can see which campaigns bring buyers, not just form fills, and spend accordingly.

The goal isn't the cheapest lead. It's the most profitable customer.

Why your brand looks like everyone else's, and how to fix it

Same fonts, same stock photos, same promises. Here's how to build a brand people remember, without a big-agency budget.

EvangelineMarketing Head, GreyLeads

Scroll through the websites of ten companies in any industry and they start to blur together: blue logos, "innovative solutions", smiling stock-photo teams. When everyone looks the same, buyers decide on price alone. A distinctive brand gives them another reason to choose you.

Why so many brands blend in

  • Copying competitors feels safe, so everyone ends up in the same place.
  • Generic promises like "quality", "trust" and "customer first" say nothing anyone else couldn't say.
  • Templates and stock photos look professional but anonymous.
  • No clear point of view: the brand tries to appeal to everyone, so it stands out to no one.

Step 1: Find the one thing only you can say

Ask your best customers why they chose you over others. The answers are often surprising and specific: "you replied on WhatsApp in ten minutes", "you explained everything in Tamil", "you actually understood our industry". That specific truth is the start of your positioning.

Step 2: Say it in your own voice

Write like you talk to customers. If you're friendly and direct in person, your website and posts should be too. A clear, human voice is one of the cheapest ways to stand out.

Step 3: Own a few visual signals

You don't need a huge design system. Pick and use consistently:

  • One or two distinctive colours that aren't your competitors' colours.
  • One typeface with character for headlines.
  • A recognisable style for photos or illustrations, ideally real photos of your team, work and customers.

Step 4: Be consistent everywhere

A brand is built by repetition. Your website, Instagram, LinkedIn, proposals, invoices and even WhatsApp profile should all feel like the same company. Consistency is what turns "nice design" into recognition.

Step 5: Show, don't claim

Instead of saying "we deliver results", show a real result. Instead of "experienced team", introduce the people. Proof is more memorable than promises.

A strong brand isn't louder. It's clearer, more consistent and more honest than the competition.

The anatomy of a landing page that converts

A section-by-section guide to the landing pages we build for ad campaigns, and why each part earns its place.

Mani KandanChief Technical Officer, GreyLeads

A landing page has one job: turn a visitor from an ad or email into a lead. Unlike a homepage, it shouldn't try to do everything. Here's the structure we use, section by section.

1. A headline that matches the ad

If the ad says "Free solar installer training in Bengaluru", the landing page headline should say almost exactly that. When visitors see the same promise they clicked on, they stay. A mismatch makes them feel lost and leave.

2. A sub-headline that adds the "how" or "why"

One or two lines explaining who it's for and the main benefit. Keep it specific: numbers, timeframes and places are more convincing than adjectives.

3. One clear call to action, above the fold

Pick one action, like "Book a free call", "Get the price list" or "Reserve your seat", and make the button stand out. Repeat the same action further down the page. Avoid competing buttons and menu links that lead visitors away.

4. A short form

Ask only for what you need to follow up. Name and phone is often enough; add one qualifying question if your sales team needs it. Every extra field costs you leads.

5. Proof near the action

Place reviews, client logos, ratings or a short case study right beside or below the form. Doubt is highest at the moment of committing; proof answers it.

6. Benefits, not features

Use three to five short blocks that explain what the visitor gets: "Get certified in 6 weeks", not "42-hour curriculum". Icons or small visuals help people scan.

7. Answers to objections

An FAQ section that handles price, time, location and "is this right for me?" removes the reasons people hesitate. Use the real questions your sales team hears.

8. A final call to action

End with a short recap of the offer and the same button. Visitors who scrolled to the bottom are interested; make the next step obvious.

Technical must-haves

  • Loads fast on mobile. Most ad traffic is mobile.
  • Tracking set up properly (Meta pixel, Google tag and conversions) so you know which ads bring leads.
  • Instant follow-up: a thank-you message, a WhatsApp reply and an alert to your team.
  • No distractions: hide the main menu or keep it minimal.
Every element on a landing page should either build trust or move the visitor towards one action. If it does neither, remove it.
